# Grant Question Patterns

Common question types, what reviewers score, and where the answer comes from.

## Organizational background

> "Briefly describe your organization's history, mission, and current programs."

**Source:** `narratives/organizational-history.md`, trimmed to the limit.
**Scored on:** credibility and relevance — does this organization plausibly do
the proposed work?
**Common failure:** spending the whole limit on founding history and leaving no
room for current capacity. Reviewers care about now.

## Statement of need

> "Describe the need or problem your project addresses."

**Source:** `narratives/need-statement.md` plus program-specific data.
**Scored on:** whether the need is documented, local, and specific.
**Common failure:** national statistics with no local data. Every applicant
cites the same national figures. Local data distinguishes the application.
**Watch:** the need must be about the population, not about the organization.
"We need funding" is not a statement of need.

## Project description

> "Describe the proposed project, including activities, timeline, and staffing."

**Source:** net-new. Requires the user.
**Scored on:** specificity and feasibility.
**Common failure:** describing the program in general instead of what this grant
would fund. Reviewers must be able to trace dollars to activities.
**Structure:** activities → who does them → frequency and duration → for whom →
over what period.

## Goals, objectives, and outcomes

> "List measurable objectives and how you will evaluate them."

**Source:** `programs/` outcomes table plus project specifics.
**Scored on:** whether objectives are measurable and the measures are real.
**Common failure:** confusing outputs with outcomes. "Serve 200 youth" is an
output. "70% of participants improve reading by one grade level" is an outcome.
Most funders want both, labeled correctly.
**Rule:** only propose measuring what the organization can actually measure. An
objective becomes a reporting obligation.

## Evaluation plan

> "How will you measure success?"

**Source:** `narratives/evaluation-approach.md`.
**Scored on:** whether a real method exists.
**Common failure:** naming instruments the organization does not use. If the
method is participant surveys and attendance records, say that plainly — an
honest simple method scores better than an implausible sophisticated one.

## Budget and narrative

> "Provide a line-item budget with justification."

**Source:** net-new, from `financials.md` for rates and indirect cost.
**Scored on:** arithmetic, allowability, reasonableness.
**Common failures:**
- Totals that do not match the amount requested in the narrative
- Budget lines with no corresponding activity in the project description
- Indirect cost above what the funder allows — check their cap
- Personnel costs without FTE percentages
- Requesting costs the funder explicitly excludes

**Always** re-add the totals rather than trusting the arithmetic in a draft.

## Sustainability

> "How will this project continue after the grant period?"

**Source:** `narratives/sustainability-plan.md`.
**Scored on:** plausibility.
**Common failure:** "We will seek additional grants." Every applicant writes
this. Better: specific diversification, earned revenue, a plan to absorb the
cost into the operating budget, or a partner commitment — with evidence.

## Organizational capacity

> "Describe your capacity to manage this grant."

**Source:** `narratives/capacity-statement.md`, `people.md`, `financials.md`.
**Scored on:** financial systems, relevant staff, track record with grants of
this size.
**Note:** if the requested amount is far above anything previously managed,
address it directly. Reviewers notice, and an unaddressed gap reads worse than
an acknowledged one with a mitigation plan.

## Collaboration

> "Describe partnerships supporting this project."

**Source:** `programs/` partners section.
**Scored on:** whether partnerships are real and documented.
**Common failure:** listing organizations with no formal relationship. If an MOU
or letter of support exists, say so. If not, describe the actual working
relationship rather than implying more.

## Diversity, equity, and inclusion

> "Describe your commitment to DEI."

**Source:** `narratives/dei-commitment.md`.
**Scored on:** concrete practice, not statements of value.
**Common failure:** values language with no practice behind it. Board and staff
composition, community involvement in program design, language access, and
accessibility are what reviewers look for.

## Word-limited variants

When the same question appears at different limits across funders, draft the
longest version first, then cut. Cutting preserves the strongest content;
expanding a short answer tends to add filler.
